# 2. Background and Related Work

This chapter situates the project across three literatures — reinforcement learning for reasoning models, the design of process-level rewards, and computational argumentation — and closes by positioning the contribution against the two most closely related lines of work. The argument developed throughout is that process-level rewards have become a crowded field in which every published signal is either a learned model or a language model acting as a judge; that argument structure is a mature formal object rather than an ad-hoc heuristic; and that the combination pursued here, a reinforcement reward computed deterministically from recovered symbolic structure, occupies an otherwise empty cell.

## 2.1 Reinforcement learning for reasoning models

Reinforcement learning entered large language model training as a post-hoc alignment step. Instruction following with human feedback [@ouyangTrainingLanguageModels2022] established the template: a reward model is fitted to human preference judgements, and the policy is optimised against it. The reward there is a learned proxy for human approval, and the training signal is correspondingly soft. The more recent turn is towards verifiable rewards, where correctness on a task with a checkable answer replaces the learned preference model, and the reasoning that produces the answer is elicited as an explicit chain of thought [@weiChainofThoughtPromptingElicits2023]. It is this chain of thought, the sequence of intermediate steps a model emits before its answer, that makes the present project possible at all: without an externalised trace there would be no structure to recover.

The optimisation itself descends from proximal policy optimisation [@schulmanProximalPolicyOptimization2017], of which group relative policy optimisation [@shaoDeepSeekMathPushingLimits2024] is the variant used here. Its defining move is to dispense with the learned value function and estimate an attempt's advantage relative to a group of attempts at the same problem. For a question, the policy samples a group of *G* outputs, each scored by a reward *r_i*; the advantage assigned to output *i* is

> Â_i = (*r_i* − μ) / σ,  where μ and σ are the mean and standard deviation of the group's rewards *r_1 … r_G*.  (2.1)

The policy is then updated to raise the probability of outputs with positive advantage. Equation (2.1) carries a consequence that is load-bearing for the reward design of Chapter 3: if every member of a group receives the same reward, the numerator *r_i* − μ is zero for every *i*, the advantage vanishes, and the question contributes no gradient. A reward that takes only a few discrete values produces such ties frequently, especially once a model is competent and most of its attempts are already correct. The structural reward proposed here is therefore continuous by construction rather than by preference; a binary structural score would fail silently, in a manner indistinguishable from the underlying idea simply not working. The argument is made at this point so that the Method chapter can refer back to it (section 3.5) rather than restate it.

The canonical demonstration that this machinery elicits genuine reasoning is DeepSeek-R1 [@deepseek-aiDeepSeekR1IncentivizingReasoning2025], which shows that outcome-only reinforcement, with no supervision of the intermediate steps, is sufficient to produce long and structured chains of thought. Its own account is explicit that the reward imposes no constraint on the reasoning process itself, only on the final answer. That is precisely the gap this project addresses: the reasoning is never checked, only its conclusion. The DeepSeek line also frames mathematics and code as established specialist domains for verifiable-reward training, in each of which an external checker (a calculator, a unit test) grounds the signal. The question pursued in this dissertation is whether argumentation can serve as a third such domain, with a recovered argument graph playing the role of the checker.

## 2.2 Process-level rewards: the crowded neighbourhood

The idea of rewarding the reasoning rather than only the answer is not new, and the neighbourhood it occupies is well populated as of 2026. The historical anchor is the distinction between outcome and process supervision [@uesatoSolvingMathWord2022], which established that a model rewarded for each correct step, rather than only for a correct final answer, learns more robust reasoning. The practical obstacle is labelling cost: process supervision requires a judgement on every step. Two responses have come to define the field. The first fits a learned process reward model to human step-level labels [@lightmanLetsVerifyStep2023]; the second generates those labels automatically, typically by sampling continuations and scoring a step by how often it leads to a correct answer [@wangMathShepherdVerifyReinforce2024]. The second removes the human from the labelling loop but not from the architecture: the reward remains a trained neural model.

The closest philosophical neighbours are rubric-based rewards [@gunjalRubricsRewardsReinforcement2025; @huangReinforcementLearningRubric2025], which score a response against a natural-language checklist of criteria. This is the line a reviewer is most likely to raise, because it shares the present project's aspiration to interpretability, and the distinction must therefore be drawn explicitly. In a rubric reward the criteria are expressed in natural language and the scoring is performed by a language model acting as a judge; the interpretability lives in the criteria, but the score itself is the opaque output of a model. Here, by contrast, the score is computed by graph algorithms over a formal ontology, with no model in the scoring loop: the criteria and the score are both inspectable, and the score is reproducible to the bit.

The remaining families confirm the pattern rather than breaking it. Metacognitive reward signals derive a score from a model's own uncertainty or self-assessment [@chenMetacognitionRewardReinforcing2026]; adversarial approaches co-train a discriminator against the reasoner [@liuGenerativeAdversarialReasoner2026]; and confidence-based methods reward progressively increasing certainty across a trace [@yoonPACRProgressivelyAscending2025]. A recent survey of reward modelling for reasoning [@panRewardModelingReinforcement2026] provides the map of this territory and, usefully for the present argument, treats reward hacking as a pervasive rather than incidental failure mode. Across all of these — learned process models, automated-label models, judge-scored rubrics, and the metacognitive, adversarial, and confidence signals — one property holds without exception: the reward is either a learned model or a language model acting as a judge. None computes its score deterministically from an explicit, inspectable representation of the argument. That absence is the space this project enters.

## 2.3 Reward hacking and Goodhart

A reward is a proxy, and optimising a proxy hard enough tends to detach it from the goal it once stood for. This is the reward-hacking or Goodhart problem, articulated early as a concrete safety concern [@amodeiConcreteProblemsAI2016], grounded formally in the divergence between proxy and true objective under optimisation [@NEURIPS2022_3d719fee], and quantified for language-model rewards as reward overoptimisation, in which held-out performance falls even as the proxy reward continues to rise [@gaoScalingLawsReward2022]. The relevance here is structural rather than decorative. A reward computed from argument structure can be gamed by producing structure-shaped text that is not better reasoning: many pairwise links that lead nowhere, or restated steps that inflate a count. Two design decisions follow directly. First, the measurements used to evaluate whether reasoning improved must be independent of the measurements used as the training signal, or any apparent improvement is circular. Second, the project reserves a dedicated analysis for how a model exploits the reward; because the reward is a diagram rather than a scalar from a black box, its exploits are legible, and cataloguing them is possible in a way that a learned reward does not permit.

## 2.4 Argumentation theory and argument mining

The claim that argument structure can serve as a reward presupposes that argument structure is a well-defined object, and it is. The modern treatment descends from Toulmin's model of argument as claims supported by grounds through warrants [@toulminUsesArgument2003], which replaced the bare premise–conclusion pair with a functional account of how support is actually conferred. For computational use, the relevant formalisation is the Argument Interchange Format [@chesnevarArgumentInterchangeFormat2006], which represents an argument as a graph of information nodes (I-nodes, the propositions) connected through scheme nodes (S-nodes) that type the relation between them: inference (RA), conflict (CA), and rephrase (MA). This ontology is the object over which the six structural measurements of Chapter 3 are defined, and it is the reason the reward can claim to be symbolic rather than heuristic: a support link is not a similarity score but a typed edge in a formal graph.

What to measure over that graph is informed by work decomposing argument quality into distinct dimensions [@wachsmuthComputationalArgumentationQuality2017], which separates, for instance, local sufficiency (whether the premises adequately support the claim) from local relevance (whether they bear on it at all). This decomposition justifies both what the reward operationalises — support density and connectivity are close relatives of sufficiency and relevance — and what it deliberately omits. In particular, the scheme-based analysis of argument [@waltonArgumentationSchemes2008], which classifies arguments by their inferential pattern, is a natural further component that this project scopes out; naming it here makes its absence a design decision rather than an oversight.

The empirical premise of the whole pipeline is that these structures can be recovered from text automatically, which is the domain of argument mining. The survey of the field [@lawrenceArgumentMiningSurvey2019] is where the project's first research question originates. It documents that argument-mining models are trained on essays, debate transcripts, and dialogue, registers characterised by explicit discourse markers, speaker turns, and rhetorical staging. A chain of thought is none of these: it is a terse, first-person, arithmetic-laden monologue with no speakers and few connectives. Whether a model trained on the former reads the latter faithfully is therefore an empirical question rather than an assumption, and Chapter 4 measures it directly. The specific tooling used is the Open Argument Mining Framework [@gemechuOpenArgumentMining2025], which standardises a pipeline of segmentation, proposition typing, and relation identification over an extended AIF (`xAIF`) representation. The relation-identification model from this framework supplies the typed edges the reward is built on; it is loaded directly from its published checkpoint rather than through the framework's containerised service (a deviation recorded as D-002), but the method and the model are those of the framework.

## 2.5 Positioning: the two axes

The contribution can be located precisely along two axes: whether the training signal is a learned or judged model or a recovered symbolic structure, and whether structure is applied through supervised fine-tuning or through a reinforcement reward. Table 2.1 places the nearest work.

Table 2.1: The two axes that locate this project. The reinforcement-reward, recovered-structure cell is unoccupied.

|  | Learned or judged signal | Recovered symbolic structure |
|---|---|---|
| **Supervised fine-tuning** | standard process supervision | [@ryuArgumentReconstructionSupervision2026; @liMitigatingHallucinationsLarge2025] |
| **Reinforcement reward** | [@gunjalRubricsRewardsReinforcement2025; @ziegenbeinTeachingLLMsHumanLike2026] | this project |

The nearest neighbour is stated verbatim, to avoid overclaiming novelty. Ziegenbein, Stahl and Wachsmuth [@ziegenbeinTeachingLLMsHumanLike2026] use group relative policy optimisation with a multi-component argument-quality reward, which is the same optimisation and a closely related reward philosophy. This project differs from theirs on both axes: their reward is computed by trained classifiers, whereas here it is recovered symbolic structure with no model in the scoring loop; and their task is argument editing, whereas here it is general-purpose reasoning. Ryu et al. [@ryuArgumentReconstructionSupervision2026] is the closest conceptual neighbour on the structure hypothesis itself, but reaches it by a different mechanism, casting argument reconstruction as a supervised fine-tuning task rather than deriving a reinforcement reward from recovered structure. Their result matters independently of their method: it is external evidence that the structure hypothesis is worth testing, which bears directly on how the null reported in Chapter 5 should be read.

Two further lines complete the picture. Argumentation has been applied to language models at inference time rather than during training, for instance by steering generation with critical questions [@castagnaCriticalQuestionsofThoughtSteeringLLM2024], and alternative pipelines exist for recovering argument structure from model outputs [@pirozelliLLMBasedSystemArgument2026]. Neither closes the training-time, reinforcement-reward cell that this project occupies. Finally, a caveat is introduced here and returned to in the Discussion: better-structured reasoning is not the same as more faithful reasoning. A chain of thought does not necessarily reflect the computation that produced the answer [@turpinLanguageModelsDont2023], so a reward that shapes the structure of a trace shapes its *appearance* of reasoning, which this project measures, and not necessarily its honesty, which it does not.
